01 · Introduction

About this project

What is motorsport culture?

Motorsport is more than a competition between drivers and machines. It brings together engineering, teamwork, strategy, entertainment and passionate fans. Different racing communities have their own traditions, identities and ways of celebrating the sport.

Through this project, our group explores the culture surrounding motorsport and shares what we have learned through research, discussion and our group presentation.

Our objective

Learn · Explore · Share

To understand motorsport culture, communicate our findings clearly and reflect on the teamwork involved in completing the project.

06 · What we learned

Final Group Reflection

Reflecting on our learning experiences, cultural discoveries, Japanese language development and teamwork.

Our Group Reflection

Our group chose Japanese motorsport culture because it brings together things many of us already enjoy: cars, motorcycles, engineering, and anime. Japan has shaped global car culture through manufacturers like Toyota, Nissan, Honda, and Yamaha; racing circuits like Suzuka; and the street-racing story in Initial D. Our presentation aimed to introduce this culture to our classmates and practise using simple Japanese in a real context. This project let us treat Japanese as a language that carries a culture, not only a subject with vocabulary lists.

We divided the work by topic. Each member took one area: Japanese cars, motorcycles, Initial D, or modification culture. We researched our own sections, then combined everything into a single set of slides. We used a shared document to keep track of sources and a group chat to give feedback. The main difficulty was coordination. Our class and assignment schedules rarely matched, so meetings were hard to arrange, and our first draft had inconsistent fonts, layouts, and levels of detail. We fixed this by agreeing on one template and having one member edit the final slides for consistency.

The research taught us more than we expected. For Japanese cars, we learned how the industry became known for reliability and fuel efficiency, as well as performance cars such as the Skyline GT-R, the Supra, and the RX-7. For motorcycles, we saw how Japanese brands dominate the global market and how competitive racing feeds back into ordinary road bikes. Initial D showed us how a manga and anime series could popularise mountain-pass driving, or “touge”, and the drift technique around the world. It also made us look more closely at tuning. We learned that modification involves engine tuning, suspension, aerodynamics, and weight reduction, and is more than just making a car look good.

Culturally, we saw that Japanese car culture reflects several values at once. Innovation shows in technologies such as rotary engines and hybrid systems. Engineering discipline shows in the attention to precision and reliability. Personal style shows in the way owners customise their cars, from understated tuning to bold designs. Passion for racing runs through all of it, from professional circuits to amateur gatherings at car meets. We also noticed that tuning is often about respect for craftsmanship, not simply showing off. That changed how we saw the culture. We had thought of it as a hobby, but it is closer to a community with its own shared standards and etiquette.

Our Japanese development was smaller than the cultural learning, but it was real. Preparing the presentation meant practising greetings such as こんにちは and はじめまして, writing self-introductions, and learning vocabulary such as 車 (kuruma), バイク (baiku), レース (rēsu) and 運転 (unten). Pronunciation was the hardest part. Long vowels, double consonants, and pitch were easy to get wrong, and we sometimes sounded unnatural when we read from our notes. Rehearsing aloud and correcting each other helped, and by the end we were noticeably more confident. We also realised that many motorsport terms are loanwords in katakana, which made some vocabulary easier to remember.

As a group, our main strength was dividing the work fairly. Everyone took responsibility for a section, and members helped each other when someone was stuck. We were also genuinely interested in the topic, which kept us motivated. Our limitations were time management, since we sometimes finished our parts close to the deadline, and integrating our research, since individual sections did not always flow together. Some of us also struggled with speaking confidently, especially in Japanese. In the future, we would set earlier internal deadlines, rehearse the whole presentation together more than once, and spend more time practising pronunciation before presenting.

In conclusion, this project gave us a clearer understanding of Japanese motorsport culture, a bit more confidence with basic Japanese, and practical teamwork experience. We learned that culture is expressed through everyday things like cars, bikes, stories and communities, and that a language is easier to remember when it is tied to something we care about. The difficulties we faced, especially in time management and coordination, were useful lessons that we can carry into future group work.
